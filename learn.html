What is Factorisation?

Factorisation means rewriting an expression as a product of simpler expressions (factors). It’s the reverse of expanding brackets.

When you factorise, you break a complicated expression into pieces that multiply together to give the original.

Common Method: Difference of Squares

One of the most useful patterns:

a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)

Example: Factorise x² − 9

x² − 9 = x² − 3² = (x − 3)(x + 3)
Tip: Always look for a perfect square on both sides of the minus sign.

Trinomials (ax² + bx + c)

For expressions like x² + 5x + 6, find two numbers that:

  • Multiply to give the last number (c)
  • Add to give the middle number (b)
x² + 5x + 6
Numbers: 2 and 3 (because 2 × 3 = 6 and 2 + 3 = 5)
→ (x + 2)(x + 3)
Factorise completely: x² − 16
